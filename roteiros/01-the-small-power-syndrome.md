### 1 · Abertura — o gancho (primeiros ~30s: retenção mora aqui)

`[TC — close, calm]`

Have you ever noticed how some people change the second they get a little bit of power?

Not real power. Not run-the-country, sign-the-checks power. A *little*.

`[BR: badge, lanyard, clipboard, a hand stamping a form]`

It can be a badge. A lanyard. A clipboard. A name tag. A job title they got last Tuesday.

And something just… happens. Almost like a switch flips. This person who was completely normal an hour ago is suddenly standing a little taller. Talking a little slower. Making you wait for absolutely no reason — because, for once, they *can*.

`[TC]`

There's a name for this. And I'd bet you've been on the receiving end of it, more than once, without ever knowing what to call it.

### 2 · A âncora + o anúncio + a virada

`[TC]`

There's this old line — you've probably heard some version of it — *"if you want to know what someone is really like, give them power."*

And for the longest time I thought that was about kings. Presidents. Big, dramatic people. But it's not. That line is about the guy at the front desk. It's about the person guarding a door that barely needs guarding.

`[TEXT: THE SMALL-POWER SYNDROME]`

So today I want to talk about what I call the small-power syndrome. And I want to ask you the question almost nobody asks — because it flips the whole thing around:

Why is it that the people with the *least* power are so often the ones who need to show it the most?

### 3 · O motor de tese — desnormalizar

`[TC]`

Now, let's be honest about what we usually tell ourselves. We say, "oh, they're just doing their job." "She's just strict." "He's a stickler for the rules." We hand these people an excuse before they even ask for one.

But is it, though? Is it really about the rules?

Because here's what I've noticed. Someone who *actually* has authority — real, secure authority — almost never needs to perform it. They bend the pointless rule. They wave you through. They make the thing easier, not harder. Real power is quiet. It has nothing to prove.

The small-power syndrome is the opposite. It's not power. It's someone *clinging* to power. And people don't cling to things they feel safe about. They cling to things they're afraid of losing.

`[TC — slower]`

So here's the reframe for the whole video: a badge like that isn't filling a role. It's filling a hole. And the smaller the real power, the bigger the hole it's covering.

### 4 · Meio — a escada de exemplos (trivial → incômodo)

`[TC]`

Let me give you the everyday ones. You'll recognize every single one.

`[BR: gate/turnstile, "please wait" sign, office corridor]`

It's the front-desk person who could clearly help you in ten seconds — and instead turns something simple into a whole ordeal, just to remind you who's holding the pen.

It's the neighbor who joined the HOA and now measures your lawn with their eyes.

It's the coworker who got promoted three weeks ago and suddenly can't say good morning without it sounding like a performance review.

It's the person who finally got the degree — and now treats everyone without one like they're a little slower, a little less. As if a piece of paper came with the right to look down.

`[TC]`

None of these people are villains in a movie. That's the unsettling part. They're normal. Until the moment they're handed that tiny bit of leverage — and you get to watch who they've been waiting to become.

### 5 · A anedota pessoal (vulnerabilidade → identificação)

> **Hani — troca por uma memória REAL tua aqui.** A DNA vive disso: a plateia perdoa qualquer coisa, menos falsidade. Deixei uma versão universal pronta pra usar como está, mas se você viveu algo parecido de verdade, conta *aquilo* — é o que cria o vínculo.

`[TC — softer, personal]`

I'll tell you where I feel this the most. It's not a boardroom. It's a government office.

Every time I have to walk into one of those places — to renew a document, to fix some tiny bureaucratic thing that ruins your whole week — I get this feeling in my stomach before I even reach the counter. Because I never know which version of the person I'm going to get. And so often, it's the version who has decided, today, that *you* are the inconvenience. That your entire existence is a form filled out wrong.

And you stand there. And you smile. And you say "thank you so much" to someone who is actively making your life harder — because they hold the one stamp you need, and you both know it.

`[TC]`

Tell me you haven't lived that. Tell me you haven't swallowed your pride at a counter, or a gate, or a front desk, because the person in front of you had one small thing you needed — and made you feel it.

### 6 · A camada da internet (transfere direto — sem caso específico)

`[TC]`

Now here's what's new. Because this syndrome just got an upgrade.

`[BR: a phone filming vertically, a hand raising a camera]`

For most of history, small power was a uniform, a counter, a stamp. Today? Today the smallest power anyone can hold is a camera.

You've seen it. Someone decides a worker did something wrong — a cashier, a barista, a teenager on their second shift — and instead of just… handling it like an adult, they lift the phone. And the tone changes. Suddenly they're louder. Braver. Performing justice for an audience that isn't even there yet.

And notice — *notice* — who they always pick. It's never someone who can fight back. It's never the person's boss, or a lawyer, or someone with more power than them. It's always the one who's cornered. The one who can't afford to lose the job. Because that's the whole point. Small power always looks for someone smaller.

`[TC — a little sharper]`

We've built a world where being *right* matters less than being *seen* being right. Where the video is the goal, and the person in it is just… collateral. That's the same syndrome. Same hole. It just found a bigger stage.

### 7 · Fechamento — o espelho, a virada final, o cuidado

`[TC — slow, warm]`

But I don't want to end this by handing you a list of people to go point at. That's easy. That's cheap. And honestly, it kind of misses the point.

Because here's the harder question. The one this whole thing is actually about.

`[TC — direct]`

Have you ever used the "do you know who I am" card? Have you ever pulled rank on someone who couldn't say no? Ever made something harder than it needed to be, for someone below you, just because that day you could?

I'm not asking to make you feel bad. I'm asking because power — big or small — doesn't change who you are. It just *reveals* who you already were. It turns the volume up on what was always there.

`[TEXT: real authority never has to prove itself]`

The people who actually have it? They never make you feel small. They don't need to. They help without making it a whole thing. They lead without stepping on anyone. A badge was never supposed to be a whip.

`[TC — the caring tip]`

So my one thing for you today isn't "go spot these people." It's softer than that, and it points both ways. Watch the signs — in them, sure. But mostly in yourself. On the days you *do* have a little power over someone — and you always will, somewhere — notice what you do with it. Because that's the real tell. Not who you are when you're desperate. Who you are when you're the one holding the stamp.

`[TC — intimate sign-off, no hard CTA]`

That's it. That's the whole thing. Take care of the people who can't do anything for you — that's where you find out who you are.

I'll see you next time.
